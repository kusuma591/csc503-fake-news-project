ABSTRACT

Fake news is a serious problem because false stories can affect public health and what people believe. Machine learning models can already label an article as real or fake, but their accuracy drops as time passes and as new topics come up. In this project we evaluate how much that accuracy drops under topic shift, temporal shift, and the two together. This part of the project reviews the work we build on. We sort existing detectors by how they handle a change in the data: static frameworks, which learn fixed patterns from one set of data and do not adapt when the topic or time period changes, and shift-adaptive frameworks, which are built to keep working as the data moves. We trace how the field reached this point and explain where our project fits. Our code and setup instructions are at https://github.com/kusuma591/csc503-fake-news-project 
INTRODUCTION

Fake news spreads fast online, and that speed is what makes it a problem. During the COVID-19 pandemic, for example, false claims about ivermectin spread widely, and people who saw them were more likely to ask for the drug because they could not tell the story was fake [Perlis et al. 2023]. False information changes how people see the world, so catching it matters. Most machine learning detectors label an article as real or fake on their own, which cuts down on manual checking, but they are usually trained on one topic from one time period [Ahmed et al. 2022]. Their accuracy falls over time and when the topic changes, because new events and words appear after training that the model never learned. Most studies look at either the topic change or the time change, not both at once. To make sense of the existing work, we sort detectors by how they cope with this kind of change: static frameworks learn fixed patterns and do not adjust when the data shifts, while shift-adaptive frameworks are built to handle a change in topic or in time. Our project takes standard detectors and measures how much their accuracy drops under topic shift, temporal shift, and the two combined. 
 
HISTORY

The problem is far older than computers. The earliest recorded case of political misinformation was around 1280 BC when Egyptians were purposefully captured to report a false location of their army to the enemy leader during the Battle of Kadesh [Choraś et al. 2020]. Deliberate falsehoods became far easier to spread after the printing press arrived in the 15th century, which let sensational and invented stories be mass-produced; a well-known example is the 1835 Great Moon Hoax, when a New York newspaper published fabricated articles about life on the Moon, and by the 1700s readers and editors had begun acting as informal fact-checkers [Soll 2016]. Propaganda was then used on a large scale during both World Wars, and the arrival of the internet in the 2000s let fake news spread faster and wider than ever.
The study of catching such content automatically grew out of deception detection in psychology and linguistics, where researchers looked for writing habits that give a liar away [Ott et al. 2011]. Those style clues became the first features for machine learning detectors, which borrowed ideas from spam filters [Sahami et al. 1998]: word counts and TF-IDF scores fed to simple classifiers like naive Bayes or SVM. The 2016 US election pushed fake news into the mainstream [Allcott and Gentzkow 2017], and a large study showing that false news travels faster and reaches more people than true news made it feel urgent [Vosoughi et al. 2018]. Shared datasets came next so methods could be tested against each other [Wang 2017]. Over time the field moved from hand-built features to features the model learns by itself, first with CNNs and RNNs [Kim 2014] and later with BERT and other pretrained models [Devlin et al. 2019; Kaliyar et al. 2021]. Once accuracy on the standard datasets stopped rising much, people noticed another problem: a detector that does well on a test set can still fail on news about new topics or from a later date. That is the problem we study.
